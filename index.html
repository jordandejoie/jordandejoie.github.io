About me

I'm a Postdoctoral Researcher in the Stress and Affective Neuroscience (SAN) Lab in the Department of Psychiatry at NYU Grossman School of Medicine, led by Candace M. Raio, Ph.D. I'm a clinical psychologist, therapist, and social neuroscientist.

My research asks how social experiences like rejection and connection change the way people value choices, rewards, and each other. I combine behavioral experiments, fMRI, psychophysiology, and computational modeling to understand social motivation, and what happens to it when social needs go unmet.

In my clinical work, I help people with anxiety, trauma, perinatal and reproductive mental health, and neurodivergence. I'm a Licensed Clinical Social Worker with more than a decade of experience across psychiatric hospitals, residential treatment, outpatient clinics, and the VA.

Research

My research investigates how social experiences, particularly social rejection, shape emotion and decision-making, and how these processes create vulnerability to psychiatric illness.

I integrate behavioral experiments, computational modeling, neuroimaging, and psychophysiology to identify the mechanisms through which social and affective signals influence learning, valuation, and choice. My years as a clinician shape both the questions I ask and how I approach them. Ultimately, I aim to inform the understanding and treatment of conditions marked by disruptions in social behavior, emotion regulation, and decision-making, including anxiety, depression, and trauma-related disorders.

Current directions

In the SAN Lab at NYU, I study how social stress shapes the way people learn from and respond to emotional experiences, and how these processes differ in social anxiety.

Areas of focus

Social rejection and belonging

How being excluded or rejected changes what people want, from a sense of control to connection with others.

Emotion and decision-making

How feelings shape the value people place on choices, rewards, and social experiences.

Stress and psychiatric vulnerability

How social stress affects the brain and body, and why some people are more vulnerable to anxiety and related conditions.

From the lab to the clinic

Using what we learn about these mechanisms to inform better ways of identifying and treating mental health conditions.

Methods

Task-based fMRI · computational modeling · behavioral economics and neuroeconomic paradigms · psychophysiology (skin conductance, facial expression analysis) · neuroimaging meta-analysis (SDM-PSI) · mixed-effects modeling · task programming in Python · analysis in R · open science practices

About me

About me

At the core of who I am and how I approach therapy is the development of relationship and connection. Because of that, I think it's important that my clients have the opportunity to know a bit about me too.

My work in this field began during my undergraduate years, when I worked with high school students and their college mentors in a program aimed at improving local graduation rates for at-risk youth. The work in this program taught me something I've carried ever since: growth tends to follow connection, not the other way around. That early lesson set me on a deliberate path. After earning my bachelor's in psychology, I went on to pursue a master's in social work from the University of Pennsylvania, where I focused on the clinical treatment of psychological disorders and trained in psychiatric hospitals and outpatient clinics serving children and teens.

The five years after I earned my degree were spent in long-term residential treatment, working with children and adolescents managing anxiety, depression, emotional dysregulation, and trauma. For most of those years, my focus narrowed to attachment-based treatment with latency-aged girls whose difficulties often stemmed from divorce, trauma, adoption, or grief. During this time, in addition to continuing my work as a clinician, I had the privilege of serving as a Clinical Director, which expanded my skill set into supervising a team of therapists and overseeing the clinical integrity of the program as a whole. My final year in residential care took me somewhere different: an outdoor adventure program for emerging adults navigating neurodivergence, including ADHD, autism, and dyslexia, often alongside anxiety or depression.

Eventually, I returned to academia to pursue my Ph.D. in clinical psychology, which I completed in May 2026. I spent the last year completing my doctoral clinical internship at the Northport VA Medical Center, where a new pull took shape: toward women's health, fertility, perinatal mental health, and the often tangled relationship between physical and psychological wellbeing. For me, this isn't only professional terrain. I live with PCOS, caught early thankfully, but still a crash course in how little is understood about its reach into mental health and fertility. I've navigated fertility challenges of my own, and motherhood only sharpened my sense of how alone women can feel in this space, and how scarce real support and education still are. That gap is exactly where I want to show up. I want to be in their corner as women navigate their health, their fertility, and their sense of self through it all.

I'm beginning my clinical postdoctoral training with TERRAP in fall 2026.

How I work

The relationship between therapist and client is the foundation everything else is built on. Before any technique or modality matters, I focus on building genuine trust and safety. That's where real change becomes possible.

I don't subscribe to a single fixed approach. Every intake includes an assessment of which modality is likely to serve a client best, and I draw from a range of evidence-based methods to fit each person's needs.
